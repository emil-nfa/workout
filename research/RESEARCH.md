# Research (M0): evidence behind the program

Written 21/11/2026 for the Workout app. Every claim the app shows points here through a citation id (for example `R-freq-2`). The app's data files put these ids in their `sources` field.

**How this was checked.** Each source below was opened at the source, not taken from a search snippet: the PubMed / PMC record or full text (read through the Europe PMC API or a browser, because PubMed blocks automated fetching), the publisher page, the preprint page, or the official web page. Where only the abstract could be read (paywalled papers), the entry says "abstract only". Text is paraphrased; nothing is copied at length.

**Confidence labels.**
- **Strong**: several meta-analyses or RCTs agree.
- **Moderate**: one good meta-analysis, or several smaller studies, with some uncertainty.
- **Expert opinion**: position stands, consensus statements, coaching practice.
- **Weak**: single small studies, observational data, reviews of gyms, or my own inference.

**Not medical advice.** This is general fitness information for a healthy young adult beginner with no injuries. Nutrition parts in particular are general guidance, not medical advice. Pain, injury or a health condition means seeing a doctor or physio.

## Q1. Verify every row of §3 (v1 research)

### R1 Frequency: how often per muscle per week?
- **Sources:** R-freq-1, R-freq-2, R-freq-3, R-vol-1.
- **Findings.** The 2016 meta-analysis (10 studies) found higher frequency beat lower frequency (effect size 0.49 vs 0.30), but most of those studies did not equate volume [R-freq-1]. The larger 2019 update (25 studies) found **no significant difference once weekly volume is equal**, in trained people and for upper and lower body; only non-equated comparisons favoured higher frequency, modestly [R-freq-2]. A 10-week RCT in trained men found 2 vs 3 days per week gave similar strength and size gains with equal volume [R-freq-3]. The 2026 Pelland meta-regression found frequency's effect on hypertrophy compatible with a negligible effect, but a clear positive effect on **strength**, with diminishing returns [R-vol-1].
- **Confidence:** strong.
- **v1 was partly wrong.** v1 said training a muscle 2×/week "beats" 1×/week. Better wording: frequency is mainly a way to **spread weekly volume** into sessions of sensible length; 2×/week is a practical default, and it helps strength a bit. It is not magic for growth.
- **Decision:** keep each muscle trained 2×/week (upper/lower rotation). It keeps sessions under 60 min and adds a little strength practice.

### R2 Volume: how many sets per muscle per week?
- **Sources:** R-vol-1, R-vol-2, R-vol-3, R-vol-4, R-vol-5, R-vol-6.
- **Publication status (checked on 21/11/2026).** The Pelland paper is **no longer a preprint**: it is published in *Sports Medicine*, volume 56, pages 481-505 (2026), online 4 December 2025, DOI 10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w. It pooled **67 studies, 2,058 participants** (79% men, mean age 25), confirming v1's numbers [R-vol-1].
- **Findings (abstract level; the journal full text is paywalled).** Sets were counted "fractionally": a set that trains the muscle directly counts 1, an indirect set (for example triceps during a bench press) counts 0.5. This counting fit the data best. More weekly sets gave more growth and more strength, with **diminishing returns**, much stronger for strength than for size. Models were adjusted for training status and study length [R-vol-1]. The older meta-regression gave about +0.37% extra growth per extra weekly set [R-vol-3]. Very low volume (under about 5 sets) gave less strength than moderate or high volume even in novices [R-vol-4]. In trained men, 12-20 weekly sets did as well as 20+ for most muscles [R-vol-5], and 9 vs 18 vs 27 biceps sets showed no significant group difference [R-vol-6].
- **The "~11 sets per session" figure.** This comes from a **different paper**, the Remmert per-session preprint (still a preprint, version 1). Its "point of undetectable outcome superiority" was about **11 fractional sets per muscle per session for hypertrophy** and **about 2 direct sets for strength**. The authors say clearly it is not a hard ceiling: data at very high per-session volumes are thin [R-vol-2].
- **Not verified.** v1's "6 → 12 sets is a clear gain" and any per-set percentage from the Pelland curve could not be checked without the full text. They are not used in the app.
- **Confidence:** strong that more volume helps with diminishing returns; moderate for exact numbers.
- **Decision:** beginners start at about 6-8 fractional sets per muscle per week (weeks 1-2), then about 10-14 for back and legs, 7-10 for chest, shoulders and arms. No session goes past ~11 fractional sets for one muscle. See PROGRAM.md for the weekly count.

### R3 Rep range and load
- **Sources:** R-load-1, R-load-2, R-beg-1.
- **Findings.** With sets taken to failure, light and heavy loads grew muscle similarly; heavier loads gave more 1-rep-max strength [R-load-1]. The 2021 network meta-analysis (28 studies, 747 adults) agreed: no load difference for hypertrophy; high and moderate loads beat low loads for strength (SMD about 0.6 and 0.35); untrained people grew more than trained ones [R-load-2]. ACSM suggests novices use loads they can lift 8-12 times [R-beg-1].
- **Confidence:** strong.
- **Decision:** v1 was right. Big lifts 6-12 reps, smaller isolation lifts 10-20 reps.

### R4 How close to failure
- **Sources:** R-fail-1, R-fail-2.
- **Findings.** In the 2024 meta-regressions, muscle growth increased as sets ended closer to failure, while strength gains were similar across a wide range of reps in reserve (RIR). The authors call the analysis exploratory because RIR was estimated from study descriptions [R-fail-1]. In trained people, 8 weeks to failure vs stopping 1-2 reps short gave the same quadriceps growth (0.181 vs 0.182 cm), while failure caused more fatigue [R-fail-2].
- **Correction:** v1 linked an FAU press release; the paper's DOI is 10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2 (*Sports Med* 2024;54:2209-31).
- **Confidence:** moderate.
- **Decision:** v1 was right. Big lifts stop at 1-2 RIR; last set of isolation lifts may go to 0-1 RIR. Weeks 1-2 at about 3 RIR while learning.

### R5 Range of motion
- **Sources:** R-rom-1, R-rom-2.
- **Findings.** Full range of motion came out slightly ahead of partial range overall (SMD 0.12, but the interval crossed zero), and "long-length partials" were not better than full range for growth (SMD -0.28, interval crossing zero) [R-rom-1]. A 2025 RCT on the lat pulldown found lengthened partials and full range gave similar results [R-rom-2].
- **Confidence:** moderate (small effects, wide intervals).
- **v1 slightly overstated it.** "The stretch at the bottom matters" is a good coaching cue, but the extra benefit of the stretched position is small and uncertain.
- **Decision:** full range of motion, controlled lowering. No partial-rep techniques in the program.

### R10 Does lifting improve flexibility?
- **Source:** R-str-7.

## Q2. Beginners: do they need less?

- **Sources:** R-beg-1, R-load-2, R-vol-4, R-split-1, R-prog-1, R-beg-2.
- **Findings.**
  - ACSM's novice advice: 2-3 days per week, loads you can lift 8-12 times, and add 2-10% load once you can beat the target reps by 1-2 [R-beg-1].
  - Untrained people grew more than trained people in the load meta-analysis [R-load-2]: early gains come easily.
  - Even novices did worse on very low volume (under ~5 weekly sets) than on moderate or high volume [R-vol-4]. So "less" should mean moderate, not tiny.
  - Adding reps or adding load gave similar results in a 2022 RCT (people with 1+ year of training; authors warn it may not apply to novices) [R-prog-1].
  - Practical side: the r/Fitness wiki calls consistency the main point of failure, warns against starting with too many days, exercises or sets, and against training to absolute failure early [R-beg-2].
- **No strong evidence found** that 10-12 sets per week is harmful for a healthy young adult. The risk is soreness and quitting, not injury.
- **Confidence:** moderate (most volume data come from trained people).
- **Decision:** 2-week ramp-in at 2 sets per exercise and about 3 RIR, then full sets. Weekly direct sets start at about 6-8 per muscle, grow to about 10-14 for back and legs by week 3. Double progression (reps first, then load).

## Q3. Which split, given an irregular schedule?

- **Sources:** R-split-1, R-freq-2, R-freq-3, R-beg-2.
- **Findings.** A 2024 meta-analysis (14 studies, 392 people) found **no difference** between split and full-body routines for strength or muscle size when volume is equal [R-split-1]. Frequency does not matter much once volume is equal [R-freq-2, R-freq-3]. Full-body 3×/week is the classic novice template [R-beg-2], but that is convention, not evidence that it is better.
- **Decision (expert opinion, my reasoning):** keep **Upper/Lower in a rotation A → B → C → D (+ optional E)**.
  - With 4-5 days, a 4-session upper/lower rotation fits 45-60 min sessions; full-body on 4-5 days would mean shorter, repetitive sessions with more set-up time per session.
  - A rotation suits the user's uneven weeks (Mon-Tue-Wed, 2 off, Sat): if he misses a day, he does the next letter, so no muscle gets skipped for a week. Fixed weekdays would break every irregular week.
  - Upper/lower alternation means back-to-back days hit different muscles (Mon upper, Tue lower, Wed upper).
  - Rule: never 4 gym days in a row without a rest day; at least 1 full rest day per week.
- **Confidence:** strong that the split itself barely matters; the choice is about practicality.

## Q4. Session length: does each session fit 45-60 min?

- **Sources:** R-rest-1, R-rest-2, R-ss-1, R-safe-3.
- **Rest evidence.** Rests over 60 s gave slightly more growth than shorter rests; beyond 90 s there was no further difference [R-rest-1]. In trained men, 3-min rests beat 1-min rests for strength and leg size [R-rest-2]. So 2 min on the big lifts and 60-90 s on small lifts is a good compromise; v1's 2.5 min on the chest press and leg press is not needed.
- **Superset evidence.** A 2025 meta-analysis (19 studies, 313 people) found supersets cut session time with **no meaningful loss** of strength (SMD 0.10) or growth (SMD -0.05). Pairing **opposing muscles** (push with pull) kept reps up; pairing the **same muscle** reduced the work done (SMD -1.08). Supersets feel harder (higher effort ratings) [R-ss-1].
- **Calculation method** (used in PROGRAM.md): one working set ≈ 45 s; set time + rest after it; ~1 min to move and set up between exercises; general warm-up 7 min; 2 ramp-up sets on the first exercise ≈ 3 min. A superset round = both sets + ~15 s to switch + one rest.
- **Result for v1 as written** (full sets, one-sided moves done left then right before resting): A ≈ 58 min, B ≈ 58, C ≈ 58, D ≈ 58, E ≈ 51. So v1 just fits, but with **no margin** for a waiting line at 18:00, and D has 7 exercises (the most set-ups). If one-sided moves (one-arm row, split squat) rest 90 s after each side, as v1's table can be read, C and D go to about 61-63 min. v1 never calculated this.
- **Decision.** Compounds rest 2 min; isolation 60-90 s. Core moves in B and D become supersets with another small move. The hanging knee raise moves from D to E. One-sided moves: left, right, then rest. New totals: A 56, B 55, C 56, D 50, E 49 minutes (weeks 1-2: 40-46 min), leaving 4-10 min of margin for a busy gym. Supersets only pair opposing or unrelated muscles, and only where both stations are close (cable stations, dumbbells), because the gym is crowded in the evening (Q11).
- **Confidence:** strong for rests and supersets; the time model is my estimate and the app should measure real times.

## Q5. Posture deep dive (rounded shoulders, forward head, upper back)

- **Sources:** R-post-1 to R-post-7, R-str-6.
- **What exercise can change.**
  - The 2024 meta-analysis on "upper crossed syndrome" (22 studies, about 900 people) found exercise improved forward head, rounded shoulder and kyphosis angles (all p = 0.001). Programs lasted **6-32 weeks**, 2-5 sessions a week, 30-70 min, and included strengthening, stretching, combined and "comprehensive" programs. It reports p-values and intervals but no pooled effect size, heterogeneity is high (I² about 75-80%) and long-term follow-up is scarce [R-post-1].
  - A 2019 meta-analysis of 10 RCTs found exercise reduced kyphosis angle (SMD -1.40); the authors suggest strengthening may matter more than stretching, without testing it directly [R-post-2].
  - The best single RCT for this profile (24 men aged 18-28): 8 weeks, 3 supervised 1-hour sessions a week. Forward head angle improved from about 47° to 40°, kyphosis from about 48° to 36°, forward shoulder from about 54° to 45°, and most of the change remained after 4 weeks without training [R-post-3].
  - The smallest change that matters after 8 weeks of corrective exercise was about 1.4° for head angle and 1.3° for shoulder angle in students [R-post-7].
- **Is "upper crossed syndrome" a valid idea?** It is a clinical label, not a proven mechanism. Critiques argue that posture and structural asymmetries are poorly linked to pain [R-post-5], and that the belief that "bad" sitting or standing causes back pain lacks strong evidence [R-post-4]. Forward head posture is linked to neck pain in adults but **not in adolescents** [R-post-6]. Experts in a 2025 consensus agreed that **stretching does not improve posture** [R-str-6].
- **Which exercises?** No trial isolates face pulls vs Y-T-W vs rows. The programs that worked combined upper-back strengthening (rows, rear delt work, lower-trap raises), deep-neck-flexor work (chin tucks) and some chest/upper-back mobility [R-post-1, R-post-3]. Ranking individual moves is **expert opinion**.
- **Honest expectations for the user.** (1) Measurable angle changes are realistic in **8-12 weeks** of regular upper-back work. (2) A strong back makes upright posture easier to hold, but how he sits most of the day is a habit, not a muscle problem. (3) His posture is not a health risk at 21; the goal is appearance and comfort, which is fine. (4) No evidence says one "correct" posture exists [R-post-4].
- **Confidence:** moderate that exercise changes posture angles; weak for which exercise is best.
- **v1 was partly wrong.** v1 cited an "Indonesian 2015-2025 systematic review": it could not be found or verified, so it is dropped. v1 framed the evening chest and thoracic stretches as posture fixes: the consensus says stretching does not fix posture [R-str-6]. Those stretches stay for comfort and shoulder range only. The "2:1 pull:push ratio" is coaching practice, not a tested number: kept as a design rule, labelled expert opinion.
- **Decision.** Upper-back work every upper session (rows, pulldown or pull-up, face pull or reverse pec deck), chin tucks and wall angels in the upper warm-up, 2+:1 pull:push sets. Posture photo check every 4 weeks in the app (side view), not a daily score.

## Q6. Core: gym or mat? Is extra ab work needed?

- **Sources:** R-core-1, R-core-2.
- **Findings.** A critical review argues that "core stability" guidelines for athletes were copied from back-pain rehab and are not justified for healthy athletes; most exercise prescriptions were never tested, and heavy multi-joint lifting already loads the trunk [R-core-1]. A 2025 meta-analysis (29 RCTs, 956 athletes aged 15-23) found core training improved core endurance (SMD 1.32), balance (0.99) and general athletic tests (1.38), but **not** sport-specific performance (0.62, not significant) or speed. Heterogeneity was very high and one implausible result (SMD 7.6) is in the data [R-core-2].
- **McGill and crunches.** The worry that repeated spine flexion (crunches) damages discs comes mainly from cadaver and animal spine studies. No study found that loaded crunches harm healthy young people. Contreras & Schoenfeld's 2011 review on this could not be retrieved (not indexed), so this point is **expert opinion**.
- **Confidence:** moderate that loaded lifts plus a little direct ab work is enough; weak for any specific "best" core exercise.
- **Decision (same as v1, now justified).** 2 short core blocks per week inside lower days: anti-rotation (Pallof press), anti-extension (dead bug), loaded flexion (cable crunch). Optional E adds carries, side plank, knee raise. **Gym or mat:** both work; cables let him add load and track progress, so the gym is the default. Home ab work is optional and not needed.

## Q7. Flexibility

- **Sources:** R-str-1 to R-str-11, R-post-1.
- **Dose.** The 2025 meta-analysis (189 studies, 6,654 adults; lead author Ingram) found long-term static stretching has a large effect (g = 0.96). Gains were maximised at about **4 min per session** and **10 min per week** of cumulative stretching (for the stretched area being measured). Intensity, weekly frequency, age, sex and training status did not change the effect. People with **poor baseline flexibility improved more** [R-str-1]. A 2018 review suggested at least ~5 min a week, on 5 days a week [R-str-3].
- **Methods.** In the 77-study meta-analysis, PNF (contract-relax) and static stretching both beat ballistic/dynamic stretching (ES about -1.28 PNF, -1.01 static, -0.55 ballistic); low vs high intensity made no difference [R-str-2]. Loaded lengthening (eccentric) training also improves flexibility [R-str-8]. Strength training equals stretching for range gains [R-str-7].
- **Long-duration stretching (Warneke).** One hour a day of calf stretching for 6 weeks raised ankle range by about 13% (knee-to-wall) to 27% (device angle), with strength +17% and calf thickness +15%; not randomised [R-str-4]. Men gained more than women [R-str-5]. So very large doses do keep working, against the "10 min plateau"; but these are a single group's calf studies with an orthosis, not practical daily routines.
- **Stretching and muscle growth.** A 2024 meta-analysis found small strength (d = 0.30) and size (d = 0.20) effects from long stretching [R-str-11], while the 2025 expert consensus says stretching does not meaningfully build muscle [R-str-6]. Not a reason to stretch for the user.
- **Morning vs evening.** Flexibility is about 3 cm better in the evening than at 8 am on the day [R-str-9]. No study shows that training at one time of day gives bigger long-term gains. **Pick the time he will stick to.**
- **Keeping gains.** After stopping, about half the gain remained (high confidence for gains, low for maintenance) [R-str-10]: it needs to be a habit.
- **Hamstring norms.** No usable sit-and-reach norms for young adult men were found (the only hit was a children's study). The app will track his own measurement (fingertip-to-toe distance, seated, legs straight) rather than compare him to a table.
- **Realistic timeline (weak evidence, from the July protocol, kept as an estimate):** touching toes in about 6-8 weeks, holding the feet in 4-6 months, chest towards knees in 18-24 months. Progress slows after the first months.
- **v1 was partly wrong.** v1 described the plateau as "per muscle" and set "mild 4-6/10 tension"; intensity simply does not matter much, so any comfortable, steady stretch works. v1's morning **dynamic** routine is fine as a habit and warm-up, but dynamic stretching gives the smallest long-term gains, so the evening static/PNF routine carries the flexibility goal.
- **Confidence:** strong for dose and methods; weak for the timeline.
- **Decision.** Evening routine ~18 min (PNF hamstrings + static holds), 5-7 days a week, intensity "strong but calm breathing, never pain". Hamstrings and calves get more than the 10-min plateau because his baseline is poor and long durations still help [R-str-1, R-str-4]. 5-min fallback for bad days. Morning dynamic routine ~6 min, optional.

## Q8. Cardio

- **Sources:** R-cardio-1 to R-cardio-5.
- **Findings.** Adding aerobic work to lifting did **not** reduce muscle growth (SMD -0.01) or max strength (-0.06) across 43 studies; it reduced explosive strength (-0.28), mainly when both were done in the same session rather than 3+ hours apart. Running vs cycling was not a significant moderator for whole-muscle size [R-cardio-1]. At the fibre level there was a small negative effect overall (SMD -0.23), and for type I fibres only with running (-0.81), but only **3 of 15 studies used running** [R-cardio-2]. Doing strength before endurance in the same session gave about 4 kg more lower-body 1RM gain [R-cardio-5]. WHO advises adults 150-300 min of moderate (or 75-150 min of vigorous) aerobic activity per week plus muscle strengthening on 2+ days [R-cardio-3]. HIIT and steady training both raise VO2max a lot; HIIT adds a little more (about 1.2 ml/kg/min) [R-cardio-4].
- **Confidence:** strong for "cardio doesn't hurt gains"; weak for running vs cycling.
- **Decision.** Count his cycling. Target 150+ min of easy-to-moderate cardio per week in total (bike rides, brisk walking, 5-10 min easy bike after lifting). If cardio is on a gym day, do it **after** weights. Running is fine; the bike stays preferred because of the (weak) fibre-level signal. HIIT is optional later (1×/week, e.g. 6 × 30 s hard on the bike), not needed now.

## Q9. Nutrition basics (not medical advice)

- **Sources:** R-nut-1 to R-nut-6.
- **Protein.** Morton 2018 (49 studies, 1,863 people): protein supplements added about 0.3 kg fat-free mass; gains stopped rising beyond **1.62 g/kg/day**, but that breakpoint is uncertain (95% CI roughly 1.03 to 2.20 g/kg/day) [R-nut-1]. ISSN: 1.4-2.0 g/kg/day, spread over meals of about 0.25 g/kg (20-40 g) [R-nut-2].
- **Creatine.** ISSN: the fastest way to load is about 0.3 g/kg/day for 5-7 days then 3-5 g/day; or simply 3-5 g/day, which fills muscle stores in 3-4 weeks. The only consistent side effect is weight gain (water); no compelling evidence of kidney harm in healthy people [R-nut-3]. 3-5 g/day is the standard dose [R-nut-4]. Optional; ask a doctor if any kidney issue.
- **Calories.** Expert review: a ~10-20% calorie surplus and gaining about 0.25-0.5% of body weight per week for novices [R-nut-5]. The ideal surplus size has never been validated in a lifting population [R-nut-6].
- **v1 was slightly wrong.** "~1.6 g/kg" was presented as a target. Better: **1.6 g/kg is the minimum to aim for, up to ~2.2 g/kg** to cover the uncertainty.
- **Confidence:** strong (protein, creatine), expert opinion (surplus).
- **Decision.** App shows: protein 1.6-2.2 g/kg/day over 3-5 meals; small surplus, weigh weekly, aim +0.25-0.5% body weight per week; creatine 3-5 g/day optional; sleep 7-9 h. All marked "general information, not medical advice".

## Q10. Injury prevention and beginner safety

- **Sources:** R-safe-1 to R-safe-5.
- **Findings.** Injury rates in bodybuilding-style training are low: about 0.24-1 injury per 1,000 hours, lower than team sports; shoulder, lower back and knee are most common, mostly strains [R-safe-4]. Specific warm-up sets improved performance; a warm-up with a few heavier reps worked better than many light reps [R-safe-3]. No study has shown upper-body warm-ups prevent injury [R-safe-2]. Holding the breath and bracing (Valsalva) raises abdominal pressure, which may help keep the spine stable, and raises blood pressure briefly; health risks in healthy people remain unconfirmed [R-safe-1]. The NHS lists urgent signs with back pain: numbness, tingling or weakness in both legs, numbness around the genitals or bottom, or bladder or bowel changes; see a GP if pain does not improve after a few weeks or stops daily life [R-safe-5].
- **Decision (app text).**
  - Warm-up: 5 min easy cardio + 3-4 moves, then 2 ramp-up sets (8 reps light, 4 reps medium) on the first exercise [R-safe-3].
  - Bracing: on heavy squats, leg press and RDLs, breathe in, brace as if about to be poked in the stomach, hold through the hard part of the rep, breathe out at the top. No long breath-holds over several reps [R-safe-1].
  - Stop the exercise for sharp or stabbing pain, pain that gets worse rep by rep, or pins and needles; normal muscle burn and next-day soreness are fine. See a physio if a pain lasts more than ~1-2 weeks or changes how he moves; urgent signs as per NHS [R-safe-5]. In France a physio (kinésithérapeute) can be seen directly; reimbursement usually needs a doctor's prescription (not verified in this research: check before relying on it).
  - Ask a Fitness Park coach to set up the leg press, chest press and back extension seat positions the first time.
- **Confidence:** moderate (rates, warm-ups); expert opinion (pain rules).

## Q12. What real beginners struggle with

- **Sources:** R-beg-2 (practical), R-fail-2, R-safe-4.
- **Findings (practical and expert opinion).** Consistency is the main reason people stop progressing; beginners take on too many days, exercises or sets; they chase weight increases at the cost of form; they think every session must be exhausting; they skip form checks [R-beg-2]. Going to failure adds fatigue without extra growth for most sets [R-fail-2]. Injury risk is low, which beginners often overestimate [R-safe-4].
- **Decision for onboarding / tips:**
  1. "The program is the one you repeat": streak based on weekly sessions, not perfect weeks.
  2. Ramp-in weeks are deliberately easy.
  3. The app decides weight changes (double progression), so he does not chase numbers.
  4. "Stop 1-2 reps before you'd fail": explained with a plain RIR scale.
  5. First week: film one set of each big lift from the side, or ask a coach to watch.
  6. Machine taken: one-tap swap to the listed fallback, remembered next time.
  7. Bring your own carabiner and a small band; go off-peak.

## Where v1 was wrong or overstated (summary)

1. Pelland is published (*Sports Med* 2026), not a preprint; frequency helps strength, not size.
2. "~11 sets per session" comes from Remmert's preprint and is not a hard plateau.
3. "2×/week beats 1×" only holds when volume is not equal.
4. 2.5-min rests are not needed; 2 min on compounds, 60-90 s on small lifts.
5. v1 never timed its sessions: they land at about 58 min with no margin for a crowded gym (61-63 min for C and D if one-sided sets rest after each side).
6. "Stretch position matters" is a small, uncertain effect.
7. Stretching does not fix posture; strengthening does the work. The Indonesian review is unverifiable and dropped.
8. Static stretch dose: intensity doesn't matter; poor baseline flexibility responds more.
9. Protein: 1.6 g/kg is the floor, not the target; 1.6-2.2 g/kg.
10. Running vs cycling difference rests on 3 running studies (weak).
11. Gym specifics: no standing calf machine, back extension bench possibly out of order, crowded at 18:00.
12. Wrong or weak links in v1: Robinson (press release instead of paper), Afonso (repository handle instead of PMC).
